Cataract & Complex Cataract

Overview

A cataract is a clouding of the natural lens inside the eye. It usually develops slowly with age and gradually makes vision blurry, dim, or faded. Colours may look less vivid, and glare from lights or sunshine can become more troublesome. Cataracts are very common, and surgery is one of the most frequently performed operations.

When to consider surgery

There is usually no rush. Surgery is worth discussing when cataracts start to interfere with everyday life, such as reading, driving, or recognising faces. Glasses may help for a while, but they cannot treat the cloudiness itself.

What the operation involves

The cloudy lens is removed through a very small incision and replaced with an artificial lens implant. It is usually done as a day case under local anaesthetic, and takes around 15-20 minutes. Most people go home shortly afterwards and use eye drops for a few weeks while the eye settles.

Choosing a lens

The lens implant can be chosen to suit your eyes and your lifestyle. Options range from standard lenses to designs that aim to reduce dependence on glasses. See our Lenses Guide.

Risks

Cataract surgery is generally safe and most people notice a good improvement, but no operation is risk-free. Possible complications include inflammation, infection, swelling of the retina or cornea, clouding of the membrane behind the lens (which can be treated with a laser, see YAG capsulotomy [link]), retinal detachment, and loss of vision. These and your individual risks will be discussed with you in detail before you decide.

Complex cataracts

Some eyes need extra planning and post surgery management. This can be the case with corneal conditions such as Fuchs’ endothelial corneal dystrophy or a history of herpes simplex keratitis, very dense (mature) cataracts, or previous laser eye surgery or injury. These eyes can carry a higher risk of complications and a less predictable result, which will be discussed with you in detail before any decision about surgery.
